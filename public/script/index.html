Survey · about 2.5 min
Slide 4

Survey: 129 responses

0:50

We ran an online survey from 22 August to 5 September and got 129 responses. Most were students between 18 and 24, who are our first users. The results were clear: 78% travel alone regularly, 75% have felt unsafe while travelling alone, 89% want the device to work without mobile internet, and 83% want a silent SOS mode.

Slide 5

What users need

0:50

When we asked for the single most important feature, the top answer was working without internet, then live location, then silent SOS. These three became the core of SafeTag. That is why the device can call and send SMS through its own SIM, sends live location every five seconds during an SOS, and has a silent SOS on a three-second hold.

Slide 6

Situations, form and adoption

0:50

Safety is not always loud. Most people said a silent alert matters most when they feel threatened or followed, or travel alone at night. Half preferred a keychain or necklace over a wristband, so we are not building a wristband. The main concerns were cost, battery life and false alarms, and that is why we added a way to cancel an SOS from the device itself. Overall, 75% said they would use or recommend SafeTag.

Changes and architecture · about 1.5 min
Slide 7

What changed since the proposal

0:45

Some decisions changed while building. We moved from a 2G module to a 4G LTE module, because 2G cannot send data during a call and 2G networks are being shut down. We used the smaller ESP32-S3 to fit a keychain-sized device. The button now has three gestures the user can change, including cancel. And during an SOS we now send live location and short audio clips, not just one SMS.

Slide 8

Architecture

0:45

The system has three tiers. The device raises the SOS on its own over 4G. The cloud, on Railway and Supabase, receives it, creates the alert and makes sure contacts are told. The Flutter app is used by the wearer, the trusted contacts and the admin. If there is no internet at all, the device skips the cloud and calls and texts the contacts directly.

Slide 10

How an SOS travels

0:50

Here is the full path of an SOS. The wearer presses the button. The device sends a small 16-byte event over 4G and keeps resending it until the server confirms. The server opens one alert, pushes a notification to every contact, and escalates by SMS one contact at a time. Contacts then follow the live location and audio. It ends when a contact resolves it or the wearer cancels it.

Personalised SOS button

0:50

This is new since the proposal. By default, a double press raises an audible SOS, holding for three seconds raises a silent one, and holding for eight seconds cancels it. The wearer can change each one in the app, and two actions can never share the same gesture. The setting is saved on the device too, so it works without a connection.

Slide 13

Backend

0:50

The backend is deployed and tested. It runs three services: the MQTT broker, the API and the job service. The database has 13 tables protected by row-level security, so each person sees only their own data and that of the people who trust them. Each device logs in with its own credentials, and a location update is only 12 bytes, which keeps it fast on a weak signal. 48 automated tests pass.

Challenges

Likely questions

The coloured dot shows who answers. Keep answers short.

Why a device? Why not just an app?

In danger, the phone is often out of reach, locked, snatched or without data. In our survey, 89% wanted it to work without internet. The device raises the alert on its own with one button, and falls back to calls and SMS.

How does it work without internet?

The device has its own SIM. If the server does not confirm the SOS, it sends SMS and makes a normal voice call to the contacts, like any mobile phone. Their numbers are stored on the device.

Is location working?

Not yet on the prototype. The 4G module has built-in GPS, but the GPS antenna is not fitted. Once we connect it, location will work. Everything else in the SOS path already works.

How long will the battery last?

Our target is several days of normal use. The device sleeps between reports and wakes instantly on a button press. We have not measured it yet; that is part of field testing after the battery and enclosure stage.

What about false alarms?

False alarms were a top concern in the survey. The wearer can cancel from the device by holding the button, or from the app, and contacts are told the wearer is safe. Gestures can also be changed so they do not trigger by accident.

How is this different from a smartwatch or existing apps?

Smartwatch SOS needs the phone or an expensive LTE watch, and most apps need the phone and data. Research devices usually send one SMS with no follow-up. SafeTag combines a cheap standalone device, escalation through contacts, live location and audio, and an app for families.

How do you keep data private and secure?

Row-level security in the database means each user sees only their own data and that of the people they trust. Each device has its own login and can only use its own MQTT topics. Location is sent often only during an SOS or a journey.

What will it cost?

Our target is under Rs 7,500 per device, working with an ordinary prepaid SIM. Cost was the top concern in the survey, so we chose parts with that limit in mind.

Your survey is mostly students. Is that a problem?

Students and young commuters are our first target group, so it fits. For elderly users we took their needs from the literature, and the pilot will include more types of users.
