Changes and architecture · about 1.5 min
Slide 7

What changed since the proposal

0:45

Some decisions changed while building, and the last column shows why. We moved from a 2G module to a 4G LTE module, because 2G cannot send data during a call and 2G networks are being shut down. We used the smaller ESP32-S3 so the device can be keychain-sized. The button now has three gestures that the user can change, including cancel. And during an SOS we send live location and short audio clips, not just one SMS.

Slide 8

Architecture

0:45

The system has three tiers. The device raises the SOS on its own over 4G, without the phone. The cloud receives it, creates the alert and makes sure every contact is told. The Flutter app is used by the wearer, the trusted contacts and the admin. If there is no internet at all, the device makes a normal phone call and sends SMS through its own SIM.

Haris

Backend · about 1 min
Slide 13

Backend

1:00

Everything you just saw runs on our backend, which is deployed and tested. It has four services: the MQTT broker that talks to the device, the API for the app, the job service that handles escalation and timers, and Redis, which holds the job queue. The database has 14 tables protected by row-level security, so each person sees only their own data and that of the people who trust them. Each device logs in with its own credentials. A location update is only 12 bytes, so it stays fast even on a weak signal. 48 automated tests pass.

Challenges

Likely questions

The coloured dot shows who answers. Keep answers short.

Why a device? Why not just an app?

In danger, the phone is often out of reach, locked, snatched or without data. The device raises the alert on its own with one button, and falls back to calls and SMS when there is no internet.

How does it work without internet?

The device has its own SIM. If the server does not confirm the SOS, it sends SMS and makes a normal voice call to the contacts, like any mobile phone. Their numbers are stored on the device.

Is location working?

Not yet on the prototype. The 4G module has built-in GPS, but the GPS antenna is not fitted. Once we connect it, location will work. Everything else in the SOS path already works.

How long will the battery last?

Our target is several days of normal use. The device sleeps between reports and wakes instantly on a button press. We will measure it in field testing, after the battery and enclosure stage.

How did you test the app?

We tested every screen on real Android phones: signing up, linking a device, adding contacts, raising and cancelling an SOS, safe zones and the gesture settings. The backend also has 48 automated tests for the device packets and the alert logic.

What about false alarms?

The wearer can cancel from the device by holding the button for eight seconds, or from the app, and contacts are told the wearer is safe. The gestures can also be changed so they do not trigger by accident.

Can contacts without the app be alerted?

Yes. Contacts without the app still get an SMS, and the device can call them directly. Contacts with the app also see the live map and hear the audio.

How do you keep data private and secure?

Row-level security in the database means each user sees only their own data and that of the people they trust. Each device has its own login and can only use its own MQTT topics. Location is sent often only during an SOS or a journey.

How is this different from a smartwatch or existing apps?

Smartwatch SOS needs the phone or an expensive LTE watch, and most apps need the phone and data. Research devices usually send one SMS with no follow-up. SafeTag combines a low-cost standalone device, escalation through contacts, live location and audio, and an app for families.

What will it cost?

Our target is under Rs 7,500 per device, working with an ordinary prepaid SIM.
