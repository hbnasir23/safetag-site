SafeTag Tech Notes

Voice notes for the viva: MQTT, how the device talks to the cloud, the packets, Railway, Redis, Celery, Supabase and the app. About 21 minutes in total.

Speed
  1. 1The big picture2:21
    Read the text

    SafeTag has three layers. The device, the cloud, and the mobile app.

    The device is the wearable. It has an E S P 32 S 3 microcontroller, which is the brain, and an A 7 6 7 0 G modem, which is a 4G L T E module with its own SIM card. So the device has its own internet connection. It does not need the phone at all.

    The cloud has two parts. The first part runs on Railway. That is where our message broker, our Python A P I, and our background jobs live. The second part is Supabase. Supabase gives us the PostgreSQL database, user login, file storage for audio recordings, and realtime updates to the app.

    The app is one Flutter app with three roles. The wearer, the trusted contact, and the admin.

    Here is the whole flow in one breath. The wearer presses the button. The device sends a small binary packet over 4G, using a protocol called M Q T T, to our broker on Railway. Our A P I is listening to the broker. It decodes the packet, and writes an alert into the Supabase database. A database trigger fires, and sends a push notification to every trusted contact through Firebase. Then our background job service sends S M S to the contacts one by one, until someone responds. Meanwhile, the device keeps sending its location every five seconds, and audio every two seconds, so contacts can follow live in the app.

    And if the device has no internet, it does not wait forever. After thirty seconds without a confirmation from the server, it sends S M S itself, and calls the first contact, using its own SIM, like a normal phone.

    If the panel asks you for one line, say this. SafeTag is a one button wearable with its own SIM, that alerts your trusted contacts through our cloud, and falls back to S M S and calls when there is no internet.

  2. 2What MQTT is and why we use it3:06
    Read the text

    M Q T T stands for Message Queuing Telemetry Transport. It is a lightweight messaging protocol made for I o T devices.

    It works on a publish and subscribe model. There is a central server called the broker. Devices and services do not talk to each other directly. They publish messages to a topic on the broker, and anyone who is subscribed to that topic receives the message. Our broker is called Mosquitto. It is open source, and it runs in a Docker container on Railway.

    Our topics look like this. safetag, slash, v 1, slash, the device serial number, slash, the kind of message. So for example, safetag slash v 1 slash S T dash 0 0 0 1 slash event. The v 1 is the protocol version.

    The device publishes on six topics. loc for location. telemetry for battery and signal. event for an S O S. status for online or offline. audio for the microphone. And ack for acknowledging commands. And the device subscribes to one topic, called cmd, where the server sends it commands.

    Now, why M Q T T and not normal H T T P? Four reasons.

    One. The device keeps one connection open. Each message has a header of only two bytes. H T T P sends hundreds of bytes of headers with every request. On a weak 4G signal and a small battery, that matters a lot.

    Two. It is two way. Because the connection is always open, the server can push a command to the device instantly. For example, S O S received, or send your location now. With H T T P, the device would have to keep asking the server again and again, which wastes data and battery.

    Three. Quality of service. We use Q o S level 1. That means every message is delivered at least once. The broker keeps resending until it gets an acknowledgement. So an S O S is never silently lost.

    Four. Last will. When the device connects, it tells the broker, if I disappear, announce that I am offline. So if the device loses power or signal, the broker automatically publishes offline for it, and the app shows the tag as offline.

    Security. Each device logs in to the broker with its own serial number as the username, and its own secret password. And the broker has an access list, so a device can only publish and subscribe on its own topics. One device cannot read or fake another device's messages.

  3. 3How the hardware connects to the cloud2:39
    Read the text

    On the board, the E S P 32 S 3 talks to the A 7 6 7 0 G modem over a serial line, called U A R T, at 1 1 5 2 0 0 baud. It talks to the modem using A T commands. A T commands are simple text commands that every cellular modem understands.

    At startup, the E S P 32 waits for the SIM card, waits for the modem to register on the mobile network, and then starts mobile data. The firmware picks the right A P N automatically from the SIM, for Jazz, Zong, Ufone or Telenor.

    The A 7 6 7 0 G has an M Q T T client built inside it. So the E S P 32 does not have to handle the internet connection itself. It just tells the modem, connect to this broker, with this username and password, subscribe to my cmd topic, and publish these bytes on this topic. The modem handles the T C P connection over 4G.

    The broker address is a Railway T C P proxy address. Railway gives our Mosquitto container a public address and port, and the device connects to that.

    The device also has Wi-Fi, because the E S P 32 S 3 has Wi-Fi built in. If a saved Wi-Fi network is in range, the device uses Wi-Fi for data instead of mobile data. But S M S and calls always go through the SIM.

    Once connected, the device publishes status online, and from then on it sends telemetry every sixty seconds, and location every five minutes when idle, or every five seconds during an S O S or a journey.

    When the device comes online, the server immediately sends it two commands. Set contacts, with up to three phone numbers, and set buttons, with the wearer's chosen gestures. The device saves both in its flash memory. That is how it can still S M S and call the right people, even later when there is no internet.

    One hardware point to remember. When the 4G modem transmits, it draws bursts of about two amps. A laptop U S B port cannot supply that, the voltage drops, and the E S P 32 resets. That is why we use a five volt, two amp supply, with a four hundred and seventy micro farad capacitor near the modem.

  4. 4The packets: what the device sends4:03
    Read the text

    We do not send J S O N text. Every message is a small, fixed length row of bytes. We call these binary packets. Why? Size. A location in J S O N is around eighty bytes. Our binary location packet is twelve bytes. Smaller packets go through faster on a weak signal, cost less data, and use less battery. Also, the E S P 32 can send a packed C structure directly, without any text processing.

    Three rules apply to every packet. First, byte zero is always the format version, which is currently 1. So we can change the format later without breaking old devices. Second, numbers are little endian, which is the E S P 32's native byte order. Third, latitude and longitude are stored as whole numbers, degrees times one million. So 24 point 9 3 3 degrees becomes 24 million 9 hundred 33 thousand. That gives about eleven centimetre precision, with no floating point.

    Now the packets.

    The location packet is twelve bytes. Version. Four bytes latitude. Four bytes longitude. One byte accuracy in metres. One byte speed in kilometres per hour. And one byte of flags. The flags say whether the G P S fix is valid, whether an S O S is active, and whether a journey is running.

    The S O S event packet is sixteen bytes. Version. A four byte event I D, which is a random number chosen once per button press. One byte type. 1 means audible S O S, 2 means silent S O S, 3 means the wearer cancelled. One byte trigger, press or hold. Then latitude, longitude, and a flag for whether the position is valid.

    The event I D is important. The device resends the exact same packet every five seconds, until the server confirms it. Because the I D is the same, the server counts it only once. So we never get duplicate alerts.

    The telemetry packet is seven bytes. Version, battery percentage, signal strength, firmware version, and flags for charging and low battery. Sent every sixty seconds.

    The status packet is two bytes. Version, and 1 for online or 0 for offline.

    The audio packet has a fourteen byte header, then the audio. The header has the event I D, a chunk number, the codec, the sample rate, and the starting state of the compressor. The audio is recorded at eight kilohertz and compressed with I M A A D P C M, which turns sixteen bit samples into four bits. That is four times smaller. Each chunk is two seconds, about eight kilobytes. And each chunk can be decoded on its own, so if one chunk is lost, the next one still plays fine.

    And the ack packet is four bytes, to confirm that a command arrived.

    In the other direction, the server sends commands on the cmd topic. Each command has a four byte header. Version, command code, and a command number. The main commands are. S O S received, which tells the device to stop resending. S O S cancelled. Locate, which means send your location now. Set contacts. Set buttons. And set Wi-Fi.

  5. 5Railway, Mosquitto, FastAPI, Celery, Redis, Supabase3:50
    Read the text

    On Railway we run four services. Mosquitto, the A P I, the jobs service, and Redis. Each one is a Docker container. Railway deploys them straight from our GitHub repository, and restarts them if they crash.

    Why Railway, and not Vercel or Supabase functions? Because the broker needs a T C P port that stays open twenty four hours a day, with devices permanently connected. Serverless platforms only handle short H T T P requests. They cannot hold an M Q T T connection. Railway runs long lived containers and gives us a T C P proxy.

    Mosquitto is the M Q T T broker. Every device connects to it.

    The A P I is written in Python with FastAPI. It is subscribed to every device topic on the broker. When a packet arrives, it decodes the bytes, checks the device, and writes to the database. Locations go into the location pings table. An S O S event creates a row in the alerts table. Audio chunks go into storage. The A P I also sends commands back to devices, and it gives the app a few endpoints, like locate now, and save button settings. And it has a WebSocket for live location.

    The jobs service runs Celery. Celery is a background task system. It runs jobs that are based on time. For example. Escalation. S M S contact number one now, then after a few minutes contact number two, and so on, until someone responds. The journey dead man switch. If a journey is still active after the expected arrival time plus a grace period, it opens an alert automatically. And marking a device offline if it has been silent for five minutes. It checks for new alerts every ten seconds.

    Redis is an in memory data store. Celery uses it as its queue, the list of jobs waiting to run. Because the jobs live in Redis and not inside the A P I, the timers survive if the A P I restarts. Redis also carries live location messages between processes, so the WebSocket can push them to the app.

    Then Supabase. Supabase is our database and more. It gives us PostgreSQL with the PostGIS extension for geographic queries, like is this point inside a safe zone. It gives us authentication, so login, sign up and email verification. It gives us row level security, which means rules inside the database decide which rows each user can see. It gives us storage, where SOS audio is kept in a private bucket. Realtime, which pushes database changes to the app instantly. And edge functions, small serverless functions, like notify alert, which sends the first push notification.

    The database has fourteen tables. The main ones are users, devices, trusted contacts, alerts, location pings, safe zones, journeys, and alert audio segments. Every table has row level security switched on.

    Two outside services. Firebase Cloud Messaging for push notifications on Android and i O S. And Twilio, which sends cloud S M S to contacts who do not have the app.

  6. 6How the cloud talks to the mobile app2:31
    Read the text

    The phone does not use M Q T T. The app is written in Flutter, and it talks to the cloud in four ways.

    One. H T T P S to Supabase. This is for normal reading and saving. Logging in, adding contacts, safe zones, journeys, viewing alerts. Every request carries the user's login token, and row level security in the database checks what that user is allowed to see.

    Two. Supabase Realtime. When something changes in the database, like a new alert, or a device going online, Supabase pushes that change to the open app instantly, over a WebSocket. So the screen updates without refreshing.

    Three. Our own A P I's WebSocket for live location. During an S O S, every location packet from the device goes into Redis, and the A P I streams it to the contact's app. That is why the marker on the map moves every few seconds.

    Four. Push notifications through Firebase Cloud Messaging. These reach the phone even when the app is closed. When a new alert is inserted into the database, a database trigger calls the notify alert edge function, which sends a push to every trusted contact. Tapping the notification opens that alert directly.

    And for contacts who do not have the app at all, the jobs service sends them an S M S through Twilio.

    Now the other direction. How does the app control the device? The app calls our A P I. For example, when the wearer changes the button gestures in settings, or a contact taps request current location. The A P I checks that this user is allowed, and then publishes a command on that device's cmd topic on the broker. The device receives it within a second, because its M Q T T connection is always open, and it answers with an ack packet, or for locate, with a location packet.

    So in short. Device to cloud is binary M Q T T. Cloud to app is H T T P S, Realtime, WebSocket and push. App to device goes through our A P I, and then M Q T T.

  7. 7One SOS, start to finish3:13
    Read the text

    Step one. The wearer double presses the button. The firmware recognises the gesture. Double press is the default for an audible S O S. Holding for three seconds is a silent S O S. And holding for eight seconds during an S O S cancels it. The wearer can change all three in the app.

    Step two. The firmware picks a random event I D, starts the buzzer and the red L E D for the audible S O S, and publishes the sixteen byte event packet on its event topic, with Q o S 1. At the same time it starts recording audio.

    Step three. The broker delivers the packet to our A P I. The A P I decodes it, finds the device and its owner, and inserts one alert into the database. If the same event I D arrives again, it is ignored. The A P I then sends the device an S O S received command, and the device stops resending.

    Step four. The new alert row fires a database trigger, which calls the notify alert function, which sends a push notification through Firebase to every trusted contact. Then the Celery jobs service starts escalation. It sends an S M S to contact number one through Twilio, and every few minutes the next contact, until someone taps I am responding, or the alert is resolved.

    Step five. While the S O S is active, the device sends a twelve byte location packet every five seconds, and a two second audio chunk continuously. Locations are saved and streamed live to the contact's map. Audio chunks are stored in the private audio bucket and played in order in the app. The buzzer stops after fifteen seconds, so the microphone can hear the wearer.

    Step six. The S O S ends when a contact resolves it, or the wearer cancels it in the app, or holds the button for eight seconds. The server sends an S O S cancelled command, and the device stops the buzzer and the audio.

    And the backup path. If the device gets no S O S received within thirty seconds, because it has no data, it sends an S M S to every saved contact, and rings contact number one for forty seconds, using its own SIM. It keeps retrying the server in the background, and queued audio is sent when the connection comes back.

    Two honest points, if they ask. Location does not work on the prototype yet, because the G P S antenna is not fitted on the modem. Once it is connected, location will work. And the device connection to the broker is not encrypted yet. The broker supports T L S on port 8 8 8 3, and switching it on is planned for the final version.
